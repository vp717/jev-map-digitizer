Main findings

OCR is the most mature area

The ICDAR MapText 2024 and 2025 competitions released word-polygon datasets and scoring code. Word detection reaches about 90% on Rumsey maps. Recognition and grouping words into place names are much weaker: the best phrase-level score in 2024 was 33 out of 100.

Point symbols are the least developed area

Most papers fine-tune YOLO or Mask R-CNN on one map series and use the box centre as the location. YOLSO (predict the centre only) and legend-prompted detection stand out. There is no large, class-labelled benchmark for topographic symbols.

U-Net is still the baseline for areas and lines

With plenty of labels, a plain U-Net is hard to beat. With few labels, MapSAM, synthetic "imitation maps", cross-edition pre-training and pseudo-labels help. Clean polygons are still hard: about 51% panoptic quality on the Paris atlas.

Legends drive geologic map extraction

The DARPA/USGS AI4CMA competition asked: given the legend swatch, find every matching polygon, line or point. DIGMAPPER is the most complete published system built on it.

Accuracy drops on a new map style

The 2024 Rumsey OCR winner fell from 76.1 to 42.3 on handwritten French registers. Released synthetic-map generators let you make training data in your own map's style.

Georeferencing mostly works from text

Recognised place names are looked up in a gazetteer (about 300 m error in one study), or printed corner coordinates are read with OCR. Few papers report accuracy in metres.

Why maps are hard for OCR

Map text is often curved or rotated, spaced very widely with symbols in between, printed in old or engraved fonts, or handwritten. It sits on busy backgrounds and changes size and style to show the feature type. One place name can be split across several words that are far apart. So the field splits into three sub-tasks: detection (a polygon around each word), recognition (the text string) and linking (grouping and ordering words into place names).

Background before the window: Strabo (USC, about 2014–2018) was an earlier map-text toolkit, since replaced by deep text spotters such as mapKurator. The original SynthMap paper (GeoAI '21) falls just before the cutoff; its successor SynthMap+ is covered in T3.

Practical takeaways for a map digitizer

Point-symbol localization

  1. Use a standard detector, but predict centres. Fine-tune YOLOv8/v10 on 512–1024 px tiles. Symbols have a fixed size per series, so YOLSO's centre + class output fits well. You want a keypoint, not a box.
  2. Stamp symbols to make training data. Paste legend glyphs onto real backgrounds with the real symbols masked out. This improved point F1 in DIGMAPPER.
  3. For many styles, use the legend as the query. One model can then handle unseen symbols. Expect weaker results on rare ones.
  4. Label smartly. Click rough centres, then snap with template matching, as YOLSO did for 22,882 points.
  5. Get coordinates from georeferenced sheets. Keep the tile offset and apply the sheet's transform to the pixel centre.
  6. Benchmarks: CaSiDD for generic signs; AI4CMA point features, scored by distance-based F1.

Areas and lines

  1. Baseline: a U-Net on tiles. It beat MapSAM with full data and beat transformers on the Paris benchmark.
  2. With few labels: MapSAM-style SAM fine-tuning, contrastive pre-training across editions, pseudo-labels from neighbouring editions, or synthetic imitation maps.
  3. For coarse national coverage, patch classification (MapReader) may be enough.
  4. For clean polygons, plan a vectorization stage (edges + watershed) and score with panoptic quality, starting from the soduco benchmark code.
  5. For lines: segment, skeletonize, vectorize, then classify by symbol style. If a modern vector network exists, check each modern road against the old map.

OCR / text spotting

  1. Start from a map spotter, not generic OCR: mapKurator + PALETTE (tiling, merging, georeferenced GeoJSON, OSM linking), or the MapText-winning DNTextSpotter pipeline with released weights.
  2. Fine-tune on your map style, using SynthMap+ or the French synthetic generator for style-matched data.
  3. Evaluate with the MapText protocol and code on the Rumsey test set. The 2025 metrics differ from 2024.
  4. Treat linking as its own step: LIGHT, or a simple MST heuristic.
  5. Post-correct with a gazetteer; the same names can drive georeferencing.
  6. Licence check: Rumsey annotations are non-commercial (CC BY-NC-SA 3.0). SynthMap+ (CC BY 4.0) and IGN 2025 (CC BY-SA 4.0) are looser.

Georeferencing and legends

  • If sheets have printed corner coordinates, read them with OCR or an LLM.
  • Otherwise geocode recognised place names, or match against already-georeferenced maps.
  • For legend-driven pipelines, LayoutLMv3 + few-shot GPT-4o reached 88% F1 on legend items.

Gaps

  • No large, multi-series, class-labelled benchmark for topographic glyphs (churches, wells, mines, triangulation points). CaSiDD is closest and mostly class-agnostic.
  • Few papers report results in metres; most report pixel IoU or F1.
  • Many promising 2025–26 works are still preprints.
